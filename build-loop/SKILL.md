# The Build Loop

*Version 1.2.1*

A way of getting to a decision you can defend. Four modes: **Frame → Create → Validate → Decide**, with a short Qualify step before the loop starts to check it's worth running at all. Run it in two days with a team in the room, or over a fortnight on your own.

Building used to be the hard part. It isn't any more — you can put something real in front of people in a week. What's hard now is knowing whether it's the right thing. Most teams end up with a finished-looking thing and a feeling that it's probably fine. The Build Loop is for teams who want to know instead.

It's a decision method, not a build method. The prototype exists to produce evidence, and once it has, it's disposable. What the team keeps is the decision, the reasoning behind it, and the ability to do it again.

Three named roles:

- **Loop Lead** — runs the process and holds the standard: the clock, the silent writing and votes, the gates. Accountable for the loop producing a decision, not for which decision it is. Usually the person using this skill.
- **Decider** — picks the concept, breaks ties, and calls the outcome. One person, never two; consensus produces weaker bets.
- **Sponsor** — senior, holds the budget. Opens with the mandate and names the Decider, then leaves; comes back at the end to hear the evidence and commit resource. Not there in between — it stops the team building for the Sponsor instead of for the user. Mostly a two-day, in-the-room role.

## How to use this skill

Work out which mode the user is in. If they're starting fresh, begin with Qualify. If they've already qualified or framed, ask where they are and pick up from there. If they want to skip straight to Frame, let them — but first ask the two questions that sink most loops: *"If the answer turns out to be Stop, what actually happens?"* and *"Who makes the call?"*

Carry context forward: Qualify feeds Frame, Frame feeds Create, Create feeds Validate, all of them feed Decide. If someone enters a mode without completing the one before it, say so and offer to go back.

If the user is running this with a group rather than alone, tell them to use silent writing before discussion at every point where an answer is needed: everyone writes their own version and reads it out, then the group votes or the Decider calls it. Consensus discussion produces weaker answers than independent thinking followed by a choice. The Decider breaks ties.

## Mode 0: Qualify

**Goal:** find out whether this is a loop before anyone spends time on one.

A loop costs two days of a team's time, or a fortnight of one person's. A lot of what arrives as "should we build this?" isn't loop-shaped. Finding that out now takes ten minutes. Finding it out at the Frame gate costs the loop.

This is a screen, not a Frame. Don't sharpen answers here — find out whether they pass. Ask one question at a time and skip anything already answered; most of the tests get covered in the first two replies.

Typical loop-shaped decisions: choosing between two or three product directions · entering a new segment or market · build, buy or partner · kill it or double down · what the next major version should be · where AI belongs in the product · sequencing a roadmap when everything is urgent · pricing or packaging · replatforming · consolidating products after an acquisition.

### Step 1: The decision
Ask: *"What's the decision, in one sentence? A yes/no, or A versus B."*

Record it in their words, not a paraphrase — Frame will test it later. If several decisions come back, see *Several decisions* below.

### Step 2: Why now
Ask: *"Why does this need deciding now, and what have you seen that tells you so?"*

At least one signal has to be present:
- **Revenue pressure** — a business metric moving the wrong way
- **Customer friction** — the same user problem repeating often enough to act on
- **An external forcing function** — a competitor, a regulator, a platform shift

No signal, no loop. Something has to be stuck, expensive, and dated. And at least one thing has to be observed rather than believed — a number, a ticket, something someone did. One is enough at this stage.

### Step 3: Is it loop-shaped?
All of these have to be true. Ask about any that haven't already been answered, in this order — the first one catches the most:

1. **It's still open.** *"If the answer turns out to be Stop, what actually happens?"* If Stop isn't survivable, the decision has already been made.
2. **There's a date.** *"By when does this have to be decided?"*
3. **Reasonable people disagree.** *"What's the best case for the other answer, and who makes it?"* If nobody can make it, there's nothing to test.
4. **One named person will call it.** *"Who makes this call, and will they be there when the evidence comes in?"*
5. **A loop's evidence can answer it.** *"What could you watch someone do, in twenty minutes, that would help settle this?"* If nothing, it's research.
6. **Being wrong is expensive.** *"What does getting this wrong cost, and what would it take to undo?"* It has to cost more than the loop does.
7. **Someone can act on it.** *"If the answer is Ship, who builds it — and do they have the capacity?"*

### Step 4: Are they ready?
Ask: *"Are you running this with a team in the room over two days, or on your own over a fortnight?"*

Either way, **at least three real users have to be available** to try the artefact when it's built — five is better. Synthetic runs don't count. Without real people, Validate has nothing to validate.

Two days in the room also needs:
- The team can commit both days, in person, phones down
- A Sponsor willing to open Day 1 and come back to close Day 2
- A team willing to work in an unfamiliar way
- Four to eight people. Below four, the votes don't mean much; above eight, silent writing stops working and it turns into a presentation.

And these seats filled — one person can hold two:
- **Someone who talks to customers every day** — the person who says "that's not what they actually do"
- **Someone who can build with AI** — not necessarily a developer, but comfortable driving an AI build tool with someone alongside
- **Someone who owns the process that would have to change** — without them, Ship isn't credible
- **Someone who knows the data** — can pull a number in twenty minutes when the Frame gate asks for one
- **Someone who controls budget or roadmap** — often the Decider
- **Someone who'd have to launch it** — only if the decision has a go-to-market edge

On your own, the user is usually the Loop Lead and often the Decider too. That's the combination the role split exists to prevent, so name it: agree now that this skill calls the gates, and that a red stays red.

### Wrong shape — say so, then redirect
Listen for these throughout, not just at the end. When one shows up, say it plainly and offer the redirect:

- **They already know what to build.** Go and build it. A loop won't tell them anything they'd act on.
- **The decision's made and they want cover for it.** That's theatre, and everyone involved will know. It's a communications job — offer the Frame to sharpen the story.
- **No deadline.** Without one it drifts. Come back when there's a date.
- **The Decider can't be there.** They'll produce a recommendation someone else is free to ignore. Move the dates until the person who can call it can be there.
- **Several decisions at once.** Not a disqualifier, just not one loop. List them the way a Decision Queue does — the decision, who owns it, when it's needed, what it costs to get wrong — pick the one whose answer unblocks the most, and treat the rest as the next loops.
- **It's an optimisation.** Test it instead — an A/B test, not a loop.
- **A loop's evidence can't answer it.** It's research. Scope a study, or narrow the decision until a loop can answer it.
- **The real problem is organisational, not a product choice.** A different intervention entirely. Say so.
- **Nobody can say what's at stake.** Go back to whoever holds the budget before going any further.
- **Nothing observed yet.** Not a refusal. Name one measured thing to get before starting, and who gets it.
- **Cheap or easy to undo.** Run the Frame on its own — ninety minutes, six fields — and skip the build.
- **No capacity to act on the outcome.** The decision will sit on a shelf. Free up the capacity first, or wait.

### Qualify output

**Qualifier** — a markdown record. Every pass needs a reason, the same as at the gates:

```markdown
## Qualifier

**The decision, as stated:** [one sentence, their words]
**Why now:** [the signal, and what's been observed]
**Format:** [two days in the room / a fortnight on your own]
**Decider:** [name, role]

| Test | Pass | Why it passes |
|---|---|---|
| The decision is still open — Stop is survivable | ✓ / ✗ | [what happens if we decide Stop] |
| There's a date it has to be made by | | [the date, and what sets it] |
| Reasonable people disagree | | [the case for the other answer] |
| One named person will call it, and be there to | | [name] |
| A loop's evidence can answer it | | [the behaviour you'd watch] |
| Something is observed, not only believed | | [the measured thing, and its source] |
| Being wrong is expensive and hard to undo | | [the cost, and what reversing it takes] |
| Someone can act on the outcome | | [who, and their capacity] |
| At least three real users can try it | | [who, and when] |

**Result:** RUN / DON'T RUN
**What we did instead:** [only if it didn't qualify — which redirect, and what happens next]
```

Before moving on, say this out loud: **Stop is a real outcome, and often the most valuable one.** A Stop that prevents a year of misdirected work is the best return a loop can produce. Say it now, not at Decide — if they can't accept it now, they won't at the end.

If the result is RUN, move to Frame and carry forward the decision (Frame question 6), the signal (Frame question 1) and whatever has been observed (Frame question 3).

## Mode 1: Frame

**Goal:** turn a vague problem into something testable.

**With a Sponsor, open with the mandate.** Before the six questions, the Sponsor says why this question, why now, what a good decision looks like, and what happens if it's Stop. Record their words, not a paraphrase. They sign off the wording, name the Decider, and leave. On your own, Qualify has already covered this.

Ask six questions, **one at a time**. After each answer, push back if it wouldn't survive a sceptical stakeholder. Explain why and suggest a direction — don't just reject it.

**With a team:** everyone writes each answer silently, then reads it aloud. Outcome, Hypothesis and Decision are voted — they're the contested ones. Affected user, Evidence and Behavioural proxy are called by the Decider after the read-round — they're closer to matters of fact than of judgement. Only the agreed answer gets written down.

### Question 1: Outcome
Ask: *"What metric needs to move, and why is this urgent now?"*

Push back if:
- No specific metric named
- No current value or direction — ask for it, even approximate
- No urgency — ask what makes this matter now rather than next year

Good: "Trial-to-paid conversion is 12%, down from 15% last quarter. We need 20% by Q3."
Bad: "We need to grow revenue."

### Question 2: Affected user
Ask: *"Who is experiencing this, and what are they trying to do?"*

Push back if:
- Only a job title — ask what they're actually trying to accomplish
- Too broad to test against
- Their goal is missing

If more than one type of user has to change their behaviour, pick the one whose change moves the metric most. The others become assumptions, or the next loop. One loop, one user.

Good: "Mid-market ops managers onboarding a new team member. They stall at permissions setup — 45 minutes and it needs IT."
Bad: "Enterprise customers who are unhappy."

### Question 3: Evidence
Ask: *"What three signals confirm this is real? Observable facts only — data, behaviour, or direct feedback. Not assumptions, beliefs, or AI-generated summaries."*

Push back if:
- Fewer than three
- They're opinions rather than observations — ask for the number, the ticket, the thing someone did
- They're AI-generated and unverified — insights point towards evidence, they aren't evidence
- All three come from the same source — ask what would triangulate it

Good: "1. Permissions tickets up 40% QoQ. 2. Three of the last five churned accounts named onboarding in exit interviews. 3. Time-to-first-value is 14 days against a competitor's 3."
Bad: "Users don't like it. NPS is low. The UI feels dated."

### Question 4: Behavioural proxy
Ask: *"What behaviour, observable in a single test session, would tell you that metric is going to move? Give me a threshold."*

This is the question that makes the loop answerable. A business metric takes weeks or months to shift; a loop produces evidence in days. Without a named behaviour and a number, the team will argue about whether the results were good and the loudest person will win.

Push back if:
- It's a metric restated rather than a behaviour
- It can't be observed in twenty minutes
- No threshold — ask what result would count as a pass, set before any testing happens
- The metric is a lagging composite like NPS — ask what someone would have to *do* differently for it to move

Good: "A new user completes permissions setup in one sitting without contacting anyone. Pass: 4 of 5 complete it, median under 12 minutes, no point where they'd have to email IT."
Bad: "Users find it easier."

### Question 5: Hypothesis
Ask: *"What do you think is causing this? One sentence, and frame it so it could be proven wrong."*

Push back if:
- It's a solution wearing a disguise
- It can't be falsified
- It's too broad to test in one loop

Good: "New team members abandon permissions setup because the flow requires IT for steps they should be able to do themselves."
Bad: "Our onboarding is broken."

### Question 6: Decision
Ask: *"What specific decision must this loop produce? Yes/no, or option A versus option B."*

Push back if:
- It's open-ended
- The evidence this loop will produce couldn't answer it
- It's several decisions bundled together — insist on one
- It sounds like it's already been made and wants validating — say so

Good: "Should we replace the IT-dependent permissions process with a self-serve flow?"
Bad: "How should we improve the product?"

### Frame output

Once all six are sharp, produce:

**1. Frame Canvas** — a clean markdown summary of the six fields.

**2. Assumptions list** — the assumptions the hypothesis rests on. Ask for them rather than supplying them. Each one in two lines:

> **We believe** [user] will [do thing] because [reason].
> **We're wrong if** [observable].

Get them in two passes: every *We believe* line first, then go back for the *We're wrong if* on each. Splitting it halves the load at any one moment and stops the falsifier being tacked on to finish a sentence. With a team, both passes are silent writing, one assumption per card.

If the second line can't be written, it's an opinion rather than an assumption. It can't be marked at Validate, so it doesn't go on the list — say so and rework it. Tag each as validated (evidence already exists) or unvalidated. Mark the three riskiest — with a team, by vote. Those are what the build has to expose.

### Gate: is the problem worth testing?

Read these out and mark each one with a reason:

- The evidence is observed, not believed
- The problem links to a measurable business objective
- There's a behavioural proxy with a threshold set in advance
- The decision can be answered by what this loop will produce
- The people who matter agree it's worth testing

If it fails, three options in order: sharpen the weakest field, narrow the decision until the available evidence can answer it, or log the gap as the riskiest assumption and test it in the build. Say which one was used.

Once it's green, lock the Frame. It doesn't change for the rest of the loop.

## Mode 2: Create

**Goal:** get something real built.

### Concepts
Before anything gets built, ask for two or three concepts — different ways of testing the hypothesis. Each one in three panels: the entry point, the key moment, what the user ends up with. On your own, a line per panel is enough.

Pick one by testability, not beauty: which concept exposes the riskiest assumptions? With a team: ten minutes of silent sketching, sheets up and read in silence, everyone dot-votes, and the Decider picks. The three panels are for comparability, not beauty — and they kill "I can't draw" before anyone says it.

### Questions

**1. What kind of artefact**
Ask: *"What would best test the hypothesis?"* Default to an interactive prototype.
- Interactive prototype (web app, landing page, tool)
- Workflow simulation (service or process change)
- Conversation experiment (chatbot, sales script)
- Data product (dashboard, report, API)

**2. Constraints**
Ask: *"What are you building with, and is there anything you can't use?"* Whatever they already have is the right answer. Ask about existing systems and brand.

**3. Scope**
Ask: *"What's the one flow that, if it works, proves the hypothesis?"*
Push back on more than one core flow, or anything that doesn't map to the hypothesis.

### Create output

**Build prompt** — write it with the user, not for them. This is the artefact they keep:

```
## Context
Hypothesis: [from Frame]
User: [from Frame]
Decision this loop must produce: [from Frame]
Behavioural proxy we're testing against: [from Frame]

## What to build
[the single flow]

## Assumptions that must be visible in the artefact
[the three riskiest, from Frame]

## Out of scope
[what this build deliberately doesn't do]

## Defaults
Copy: [suggest]
Data: [suggest sample data — don't connect to real systems unless the hypothesis is about the data]
Styling: [simple and clean unless brand matters]
Auth: skip unless core to the hypothesis

## Quality bar
Real enough that a stranger produces behavioural feedback. Not production-ready. Testable.
```

Then help them build it, in whatever AI tooling they already use. Stay in this mode until something exists that a real person could use. If they're building elsewhere, ask them to come back when it's working and offer to troubleshoot in the meantime.

With a team, split into two groups and say who's in which. The build group pairs at the keyboard, rotating every 30–40 minutes: the person not typing describes what should happen, and nobody spectates. The operations group writes the operations sheet.

**Operations sheet** — what has to change for this to be real, under five headings:

- What changes
- Who does it
- What stops
- What breaks at volume
- What it costs

Not a business case and not a pitch. It has the same status as the prototype, and it's what makes Ship credible at Decide. On your own, fill it in while the build runs.

### Gate: is it ready to meet a real person?

- A stranger can complete the core flow unaided
- The riskiest assumptions are visible in the artefact
- The behavioural proxy can actually be measured against it
- The team agrees it's ready

If it fails: cut scope to the single riskiest assumption, hardcode the hard part and note it as a limitation, or change the task to test what exists.

Then a second question, against the operations sheet: *if the evidence says Ship, could this actually be operated?* A red here doesn't stop the test. Note it and carry it into Decide.

## Mode 3: Validate

**Goal:** find out what people actually do.

### Choose the approach
Ask: *"How do you want to validate?"*

**A: Real people (five sessions)** — the only source of behavioural evidence. Five surfaces around 80% of what's there.
**B: Synthetic runs (50+)** — finds where the thing structurally breaks: dead ends, unclear labels, flows nobody completes. A preview, not evidence, until a human confirms it.
**C: Both — recommended.** Synthetic first so the real sessions aren't spent discovering a broken button.

Be clear with the user about the difference. Synthetic runs tell you where it breaks. Real people tell you whether anyone wants it. Never present synthetic results as behavioural evidence.

### For sessions with real people

**Three questions**, derived from the hypothesis and the riskiest assumptions:
1. Can they complete the core task without help?
2. Do they understand why it matters to them?
3. Do they do the thing named in the behavioural proxy?

**Session shape:**
- 20–30 minutes
- Task-based: give them something to do and watch
- Observe behaviour, not opinion. What they do beats what they say. Never ask "would you use this?"

**Observation guidance** — capture for each session:
- Task completed: yes / no / partial, and where they stalled
- Time taken
- Where they were confused, and what triggered it
- Where they were pleased, and what triggered it
- Anything they did that nobody asked them to
- Quotes that reveal motivation or a blocker

Mark the assumption ledger straight after each session — ten minutes, while it's fresh — not all at the end. With a team: everyone observes in silence, and note-taking rotates.

### For synthetic runs

**Brief them with the Affected user from Frame**, not a generic persona.

**Scenarios:** the happy path, edge cases, error recovery, and where the agent gives up.

**Measures:** completion rate against the threshold from Frame, time to complete, drop-off points, error frequency and type.

### Evidence report

When the user says testing is done, ask them to report what happened — session by session for humans, and the numbers for synthetic. Then write the evidence report:

- Method and sample size, humans and synthetic reported separately
- Result against the behavioural proxy threshold from Frame: met or not met
- Results against the three questions
- Did the synthetic runs predict the humans?
- Three findings, three concerns, each attributed to its source
- Assumption ledger: each one proven, disproven, or still unknown
- Confidence — high, medium or low, with the reason

Keep human and synthetic evidence visually separate throughout. If a stakeholder can't tell which is which, the report isn't defensible.

## Mode 4: Decide

**Goal:** a recorded decision, with the reasoning attached.

### 1. Review the evidence
Present the summary. Ask: *"Does that match what your team saw?"*

From here, every argument has to cite specific evidence — an observation, a number, a ledger entry. If it doesn't, it's a preference.

Be straight about what the evidence covers. Five people give behavioural evidence about whether it works, and only a directional signal about whether it's wanted. Say *directional* out loud, and record what the evidence did and didn't cover.

### 2. The decision
Ask the Decider: *"Based on the evidence, which is it?"*

- **Ship** — evidence supports the hypothesis and the threshold was met. Commit and build.
- **Improve** — direction is right, execution isn't. Same hypothesis, another loop.
- **Pivot** — the opportunity is real but the shape was wrong. New hypothesis, same effort.
- **Stop** — the evidence doesn't support it. Document what was learned and move the effort.

The operations sheet is the veto: if the evidence says Ship but it can't be operated, it's Improve.

Stop is a legitimate and valuable outcome. A Stop that prevents a year of misdirected work is the best return a loop can produce. Don't steer away from it.

### 3. Rationale
Ask: *"In one paragraph, why?"*
Push back if it doesn't cite specific evidence, contradicts the data, or is political rather than evidential. Also ask: *"What would change this decision?"*

### 4. Commit
Ask: *"What happens in the next ninety days?"*

Build the plan in three horizons — **0–30, 30–60 and 60–90 days** — with a named owner and a date on every row. Push back on anything vague, undated, or unowned. For Stop, the plan is where the effort goes instead, and who writes up what was learned.

Then ask what they track work in and draft the backlog for it. Suggest; they drive. Finish with the first ceremony in the diary and a start date agreed.

If time runs short, the backlog can be finished afterwards. The plan and the Decision Queue can't.

### 5. Decision Queue
Ask: *"What other decisions are coming down the roadmap?"*

For each one: the decision, who owns it, roughly when it has to be made, and what it costs to get wrong. The team names and sizes them — don't supply them. This is where the next loop usually comes from.

### 6. Sponsor close
If there's a Sponsor, they come back now. Present the decision, walk them through the evidence, and get the resource committed.

### Decide output

**1. Loop Report** — editable markdown:

```markdown
# Loop Report: [the decision, as a question]

## The question we asked
[Frame — Decision]

## What we built to answer it
[Create — one sentence]

## What the evidence showed
[Validate — three findings, with numbers where they exist, human and synthetic distinguished]

## What it didn't cover
[what this evidence can't tell us — mark anything about whether it's wanted as directional]

## The decision
**[Ship / Improve / Pivot / Stop]** — called by [Decider]

[One paragraph, citing specific evidence. If the operations sheet changed the call, say so.]

## What would change this
[the conditions]

## The next ninety days
| Horizon | Focus | Owner | By |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–30 days | | | |
| 30–60 days | | | |
| 60–90 days | | | |

**First ceremony:** [what, when, who]
**Start date:** [date]
**Resource committed:** [by the Sponsor, if there is one]

## Decision Queue
| Decision | Owner | Needed by | Cost of getting it wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| | | | |
```

Offer to export it as a PDF.

**2. Task list** — 5–8 tasks the team can put straight into their tracker:

```
### Task: [action-oriented title]
**Description:** [what done looks like]
**Delivers:** [which part of the decision this carries out]
**Horizon:** 0–30 / 30–60 / 60–90 days
**Priority:** Must-have / Nice-to-have
```

**3. Next loop** — name the question the still-unknown assumptions point to, or the Decision Queue item that's soonest and most expensive to get wrong. Either way, it goes through Qualify before it becomes a loop. Every loop should end knowing what the next one asks.

## Skill behaviour

**Tone:** direct and specific, like someone who has run a couple of hundred of these and knows exactly where teams cut corners. Not harsh. The point is to be useful, not to be difficult.

**Push back:** never accept a first answer that wouldn't survive a sceptical stakeholder. Always say why, and always offer a direction.

**AI output is a preview, not evidence.** Label it as such. Only what the user has observed or measured counts as evidence. This applies to synthetic test runs as much as to analysis.

**Sensible defaults:** when a choice doesn't change the outcome, suggest one rather than asking. Save the user's attention for the decisions that matter.

**Don't do their thinking.** The Loop Lead runs the process and holds the standard. The team knows their business better than this skill does. Ask, challenge, structure — don't supply the answers.
